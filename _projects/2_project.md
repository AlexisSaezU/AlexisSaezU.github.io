One of the most puzzling observations in induced seismicity is the occurrence of earthquakes **days to months after injection operations have ceased**. This post-injection seismicity poses significant challenges for hazard assessment because seismic events can occur unexpectedly long after the injection source has been shut off. My research provides a physical explanation for this phenomenon based on the mechanics of aseismic fault slip.

During fluid injection, elevated pore pressures can reactivate faults and induce slow, stable sliding known as **aseismic slip**. Even after injection stops, this aseismic slip continues to propagate as a **pulse-like rupture** that travels outward from the injection zone. As this slip pulse advances, it transfers elastic stresses to the surrounding rock, including to nearby faults that were not directly affected by the injected fluids.

The figure illustrates how the aseismic slip on the reactivated fault generates a characteristic pattern of **Coulomb stress changes** in the surrounding medium. Positive stress lobes (shown in red/yellow) extend ahead of the slip front, loading adjacent "receiver" faults that may be critically stressed. When the cumulative stress transfer exceeds the fault's strength, earthquakes are triggered—even though fluid pressure at that location may have already dissipated.

This **stress-transfer mechanism** explains several puzzling features of post-injection seismicity, including its delayed onset, its occurrence at distances beyond the direct reach of pressure diffusion, and its tendency to form swarm-like sequences. My work demonstrates that aseismic slip acts as a long-range stress mediator, enabling seismicity to be triggered far from the injection source both in space and time, with important implications for induced seismicity forecasting and traffic-light protocols.
