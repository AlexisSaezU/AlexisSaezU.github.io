Fluid injection into the subsurface can reactivate pre-existing faults, triggering slip that may manifest as induced seismicity. Understanding the mechanics of how pressurized fluids drive fault slip is essential for managing seismic hazards associated with underground operations such as wastewater disposal, geothermal energy extraction, and CO₂ sequestration.

In my research, I investigate the fundamental physics of **three-dimensional fluid-driven frictional ruptures** that propagate along pre-existing faults. When fluid is injected at a point source, the resulting fault slip grows as an expanding rupture front whose shape is controlled by the interplay between fluid pressure diffusion and frictional resistance. Using a combination of analytical theory and numerical simulations, I have shown that these ruptures exhibit **self-similar growth**, meaning their shape remains constant in time when scaled by the rupture dimensions.

A key finding of this work is that the rupture front develops an **elongated, nearly elliptical shape** that depends on the orientation of the ambient stress field and the fault's frictional properties. The rupture grows faster in the direction of maximum resolved shear stress, leading to significant anisotropy in the rupture dimensions. I have derived analytical solutions that predict the rupture shape and the slip distribution, providing valuable benchmarks for numerical models and practical tools for estimating the extent of fault reactivation during injection operations.

This research provides fundamental insights into the conditions under which fluid-driven fault slip remains stable (aseismic) versus becoming unstable (seismic), with direct implications for the assessment and mitigation of induced seismicity risks.
