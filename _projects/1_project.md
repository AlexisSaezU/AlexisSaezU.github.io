Fluid injection into the subsurface can reactivate pre-existing faults, triggering slip that may manifest as induced seismicity or slow slip. Understanding the mechanics of how pressurized fluids drive fault slip is essential for managing seismic hazards associated with underground operations such as wastewater disposal, geothermal energy extraction, and CO₂ sequestration.

In my first PhD paper, we investigated the fundamental mechanics of three-dimensional fluid-driven stable frictional ruptures that propagate along pre-existing faults. When fluid is injected in a fault, the resulting slip grows as an expanding rupture whose speed is controlled by the interplay between the injection potency and fault initial stress state. Using a combination of analytical techniques and numerical simulations, we showed that these ruptures exhibit self-similar growth when driven by injection at a constant volumetric rate, meaning their shape remains constant in time when scaled by the rupture dimensions.

A key finding of this work is that the rupture front develops an elongated, nearly elliptical shape that depends on the orientation of the ambient stress field and the fault's frictional properties. The rupture grows faster in the direction of maximum resolved shear stress, leading to significant anisotropy in the rupture dimensions. I have derived analytical solutions that predict the rupture shape and the slip distribution, providing valuable benchmarks for numerical models and practical tools for estimating the extent of fault reactivation during injection operations.

This research provides fundamental insights into the conditions under which fluid-driven fault slip remains stable (aseismic) versus becoming unstable (seismic), with direct implications for the assessment and mitigation of induced seismicity risks.
